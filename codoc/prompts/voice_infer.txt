A person gave feedback on prose that codoc had generated for their feature tree.
Your job is to say what that feedback teaches about HOW THEY WRITE, so later
passes write that way the first time.

You are shown one or more pieces of feedback, of two kinds:

- A **rewrite** has `before` (what codoc wrote) and `after` (what the person left
  in its place). The preference is not stated anywhere; you infer it from the gap.
- A **note** has `note` (what the person typed) and usually `quoted` (the sentence
  of ours they typed it on). Here the preference is STATED, so do not go looking
  for it in a diff — there is none. Read what they asked for.

For each, decide what it was about, and only then draw a lesson.

## First, classify the feedback

- `content` — the facts changed. They fixed something that was WRONG, or added
  information that was missing, or removed a claim that was not true. Teaches
  nothing about voice.
- `style` — the same facts, said differently. Reordered, shortened, plainer words,
  a different opening, a threshold spelled out instead of gestured at.
- `mixed` — both at once, which is the common case: somebody correcting a claim
  tidies the sentence while they are in there.
- `noise` — a typo, a whitespace change, one word swapped, a link fixed. Too
  small to carry a preference.

**Be strict about `content`.** The most damaging mistake you can make here is
reading a factual correction as a preference — that turns one repair into an
instruction to assert that same fact everywhere. If the before and after disagree
about what the code DOES, it is `content` (or `mixed`), never `style`.

A note is classified the same four ways, by what it ASKS FOR:

- `Loop B drains this, not Loop A` is `content` — a wrong fact, reported in words.
- `don't call these handlers, they drain the queue` is `style`: same code, their
  word for it. So is `this opens on the class name again` and `say what it is for`.
- `make the retry back off` is a request to change the CODE, not the prose. Nothing
  about writing is being corrected, so classify it `content` and draw no lesson.
- `no`, `hm`, `see above` — `noise`.

## Then, draw at most one lesson per piece of feedback

Only `style` and `mixed` feedback yields a lesson, and for a `mixed` one the lesson
must come from the part that did not change the facts. A piece of feedback may also
yield no lesson: if you cannot name the preference in one sentence a writer could
act on, return none for it. That is a normal outcome and much better than a vague
one.

For a note, the lesson is the author's own request generalized **exactly as far as
they put it and no further**. `don't call these handlers` supports `Call this kind
of thing what the codebase calls it; never "handler"` — it does not support `Avoid
abstract nouns`. A note is one sentence about one paragraph, which makes
over-reading it easy; if the request only makes sense for the feature you were
shown, there is no lesson.

A lesson has:

- `axis` — one of `altitude` (how far above the mechanism to write), `length`
  (sentence and paragraph size), `vocabulary` (the words this author uses and
  refuses), `structure` (what the first sentence does, what order things come in),
  `specificity` (how much naming of symbols, values, thresholds), `titling` (how a
  feature gets named).
- `instruction` — the preference as ONE sentence addressed to the writer, in the
  imperative. It goes into a later prompt verbatim, so write it as guidance and
  not as an observation about a person. Say `Open on the problem the caller has,
  not on the name of the module`, never `The author prefers purpose-first
  openings`.
- `axis_detail` — three or four words naming the direction, for a reader
  skimming a list (`shorter sentences`, `names thresholds`, `avoids I/O jargon`).

**Generalize only as far as the evidence reaches.** One rewrite that cut a
sentence supports `Stop after the rule; do not add a third sentence unless it
carries a threshold or a name` and does NOT support `Write tersely`. A lesson that
would change every description in the tree needs to be visible in the rewrite you
were shown, not inferred from one instance of it.

**Never draw a lesson about the subject matter.** `Mention that the store is
SQLite` is a fact about this codebase, not a preference about writing. If the
instruction only makes sense for the one feature you were shown, it is not a
lesson.
