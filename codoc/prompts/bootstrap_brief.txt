You are about to write a **feature tree** for the codebase "{repo_name}" — a
navigable hierarchy of what the code is *for*.

Before describing any part of it, read what the project already says about
itself and form a picture of the whole. That picture is then handed to every
later pass, so a file gets described as a part of something rather than on its
own terms.

This is the only pass that sees the project's own prose. Later passes see code.

## What you are reading

The project's README, its package metadata, and the module docstrings and
leading comments of every source file. That is where an author writes down the
things the code cannot say: what the program is for, who its output is for, what
it deliberately does not do, and why a rule went one way rather than the other.

Take those statements at face value. They are the author speaking about their own
work, and they are evidence in exactly the way a commit message is.

## What to produce

A short brief, in plain everyday words, that a reader who has never seen this
codebase could hold in their head while reading any one file of it.

- `purpose`: what this program is for, in one or two sentences. Say what goes in
  and what comes out. Avoid the project's own jargon unless you also define it.
- `audience`: who the output is for, and what they will do with it, when the
  project says. This decides more design questions than anything else, so it is
  worth stating separately.
- `out_of_scope`: things a reader might reasonably expect this program to do and
  it does not. Only what the project actually says or clearly implies.
- `vocabulary`: words this codebase uses in a particular way, each with a short
  gloss. A word somebody would look up, or misread, or would not otherwise
  connect to the thing it names.
- `decisions`: the design choices this project made where a different project
  could sensibly have chosen otherwise, each with the reason if one is given.
  **This is the most valuable part of the brief and the easiest to leave empty.**
  A rule stated with a reason — "the hyphen is dropped because in a justified
  column the typesetter put it there" — is a decision. So is a threshold with a
  justification, an order that is described as mattering, or a stated cost.
  Where the text names what was given up, say that too.
- `ordering`: any place the project says one step must run before another, and
  what would go wrong otherwise. These are where a later change breaks something
  far away, and they are almost never visible from one file.

## Rules

- Only what the text supports. If the project never says who the output is for,
  leave `audience` empty rather than guessing from the domain.
- No invention: no rejected alternatives nobody mentioned, no history nobody
  wrote down, no "the team decided".
- Plain words. A reader should understand every line on a first read.
- Write every field as prose a person would say out loud, not as labels.
- Empty is an honest answer for any field. A short true brief beats a long one
  with a guess in it.
