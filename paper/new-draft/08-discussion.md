# 8. Discussion

The representation functioned as a communication medium rather than a comprehension aid. Participants did not use the tree to understand how the code works. They used it to see what was decided, direct what should change, and record their own verdicts. That reframing, from comprehension tool to communication layer, is a consequence of a more fundamental finding. A representation that a developer trusts becomes the surface they communicate through, because they are willing to write into it and reason from what it says. A representation they do not trust cannot serve that function regardless of its expressive power.

## 8.1 The Trust Threshold as a Design Principle

The most striking behavioral shift occurred not when participants discovered a feature but when they stopped verifying one. Early in their sessions, most participants cross-checked the tree against the code. After two or three such checks, they stopped opening files entirely and reasoned from the tree alone.

This suggests a threshold effect rather than a gradient. Section 7.4 reports the pattern and acknowledges the alternative that think-aloud narration may impose apparent discreteness on what is a rapid but continuous decline. The design argument that follows holds regardless of whether the transition is genuinely discontinuous or merely fast enough to appear so within a twenty-minute session, because both readings imply the same architectural consequence. The representation becomes useful not when it gains capabilities but when it crosses a trust boundary. Below that boundary, every affordance the tool offers is wasted because the developer verifies everything against the source anyway. Above it, even a minimal representation carries substantial value, because each lookup that does not require opening a file saves a context switch, and those savings accumulate across the session.

An alternative explanation is satisficing under time pressure. Two observations argue against it. Satisficing predicts a gradual decline in verification rate, but we observed a rapid transition within a single minute rather than steady attrition across the session. And the same participants under the same time pressure in the baseline condition continued checking throughout. The between-condition difference suggests that what changed is the informativeness of each check rather than the budget for checking.

A second alternative is task sufficiency: participants stopped opening files not because they trusted the tree but because they had gathered enough orientation to act. Two asymmetries favor the trust account. Pure sufficiency predicts that exploration stops in general, but participants selectively stopped consulting code while increasing their use of the tree, reading new features, following bindings, and issuing comments. They abandoned one information source while deepening their engagement with another, and that selectivity is what trust in a representation looks like. The initial checking phase also distinguishes the accounts. The tree is equally navigable on the first minute as on the fifth, so if the explanation were efficiency, the checking phase would serve no purpose. It exists because participants were testing whether the tree was reliable enough to navigate by.

The trust threshold inverts the conventional trajectory of developer tools. A tool with three features the developer trusts is worth more than one with thirty they do not, because an untrusted tool is just another thing to verify. Investment in faithfulness infrastructure pays off more than investment in expressivity. If the loops fail once, if one description contradicts the code, the developer drops below the threshold and the tool becomes inert. Recovery follows the same path as initial formation because the same verification mechanism that builds trust also rebuilds it, but Lee and See [2004] found that trust rebuilds more slowly than it forms because the developer now knows the mechanism can fail and must test more features before the induction feels safe again.

Parasuraman and Riley's [1997] taxonomy names what happens on each side. Below the threshold, *disuse* dominates. Above it, the risk shifts to *misuse*, over-reliance on a description that may have drifted. The synchronization loops address both. Verifiable structure enables the rapid calibration that ends disuse, and continuous maintenance prevents the silent drift that causes misuse.

Schemmer et al.'s [2022] distinction between *attitudinal* and *behavioral* trust clarifies the mechanism. Attitudinal trust is "I believe this is probably right." Behavioral trust is "I will make my next decision from the tree without checking" and collapses on a single failure. The trust threshold IS the formation of behavioral trust. The formation reproduces Bainbridge's [1983] irony of automation at the representation level. The more reliable the tree becomes, the less the developer verifies, and the more dependent they become on its continued accuracy. Every decision made after the threshold was made without independent verification, so a single stale description discovered later retroactively undermines all decisions that relied on it. The synchronization loops are the architectural response to that irony, ensuring reliability remains justified even as monitoring declines.

We theorize three structural conditions that enable rapid threshold formation, grounded in the observed behavior but not experimentally isolated from it. The study establishes the behavioral shift and its correlation with the architecture but cannot prove that these specific conditions caused it. Verification must be *local*, so a developer can check one feature by opening its bound code without reading the rest. The maintenance mechanism must be *uniform*, so verifying any two features provides evidence about all the rest. And verification must be *cheap*, taking seconds rather than minutes so that checking is cheaper than not checking. Each condition makes a negative prediction. Without locality, a developer must read the entire representation to verify one claim, and the cost scales with the artifact's size rather than staying bounded. Without uniformity, verifying two features provides evidence about only those two, and the developer must check proportionally. Without cheapness, the expected cost of a single check exceeds the expected benefit, and the Sonar [2026] gap applies: 96% of developers report not fully trusting AI-generated code yet only 48% verify before committing. When any condition breaks, the threshold cannot form.

The "uniform" condition explains why two or three checks suffice where fifty might not. Khati et al. [2025] found that developer trust in AI is task-specific, varying across code tasks because the mechanism that produces correct output in one context has no guaranteed relation to the mechanism in another. codoc's response is architectural uniformity. The same content-hash comparison maintains every binding regardless of feature type, so verifying the mechanism on any feature provides evidence about all features. Dzindolet et al. [2003] showed that operators who understand an automation's mechanism calibrate trust faster than those who observe only its outputs. Three observations from Section 7.4 support a mechanism-testing interpretation. First, participants verbalized hypotheses about the synchronization machinery rather than about specific features, targeting the binding mechanism rather than individual content. Second, participants chose features at different hierarchical depths rather than adjacent ones, strategic sampling of the mechanism's reach. Third, baseline participants under identical time pressure continued checking throughout, arguing against time-budget exhaustion. The induction from "these two are right" to "the rest are probably right" is rational if the developer is testing the process rather than sampling the population.

Vella and Blincoe [2026] confirmed longitudinally that developer trust in AI coding tools shifts continuously rather than stabilizing, implying that the architecture supporting trust must itself be continuous rather than relying on a one-time calibration event.

That trust forms is not itself a contribution; it is the definition of the phenomenon. What the existing literature does not specify is what properties of a *representation's design* determine how quickly a developer accumulates enough evidence to delegate, and the observation that "people stop checking correct things" does not answer it because it says nothing about the cost structure that determines whether checking happens at all. Baltes, Speith et al. [2026] identified a "significant maturity gap" in how SE research engages with trust, recommending established models over per-tool reinvention. The trust threshold follows that recommendation. What it adds is the identification that verification cost is the binding constraint on tool utility, and the three designable conditions under which that cost falls below the level at which checking becomes default behavior. This has a falsifiable prediction: a tool that is maximally expressive but expensive to verify will not cross the threshold, while a tool that is minimally expressive but cheap to verify will. The prediction is architectural rather than cognitive. It does not claim to explain how trust works in the mind, only what infrastructure makes the behavioral transition happen faster.

## 8.2 What Trust Enables, Communication Through the Representation

Once across the threshold, participants used codoc to *communicate* rather than to *understand*. Toward the agent, communication took the form of comments and steers scoped to a feature. Toward their own future selves, it took the form of verdicts and description edits that outlived the session. Trust in the medium is a necessary condition for this pattern, though the study cannot isolate it from the communication affordances that trust unlocks. A developer who believes their edit will be overwritten on the next agent pass has no reason to write. The hold set protects human edits from automation, so writing into the tree is meaningful rather than futile. Communication was concentrated in the period after participants stopped cross-checking, consistent with a trust-first direction, though the study did not code temporal ordering finely enough to prove the causal sequence. We cannot claim trust is sufficient for the shift to structural communication, since the feature-scoped comment affordance itself may have shaped the behavior independently of trust. The bundle comparison limitation from Section 9.1 applies here as elsewhere.

This reframes what a codebase representation is for. Prior work on code comprehension tools assumes the developer's goal is to build a mental model of how the code works. As agents become capable enough to write acceptable code, the dominant task shifts from construction to oversight. Kirsh [2010] argues that external representations do not merely offload memory but change the cognitive task itself. Reading a feature tree is performing *orientation*, locating oneself in a structure. Reading implementation is performing *comprehension*. The feature tree makes orientation cheap so that comprehension can be targeted to the few features that matter.

The feature tree functions as what Star and Griesemer [1989] call a *boundary object*. The developer reads it as "what this system is for," the agent as "what code to touch," and a future auditor as "what was decided and by whom." These readings coexist on the same text without requiring consensus on interpretation. The structural communication pattern from Section 7.2 is a behavioral consequence of this property. A participant who wrote "this feature should not depend on X" expressed intent at their own level of abstraction, and the system's translation into a scoped directive was the agent's reading of the same text. Neither party needed to speak the other's language. Accepting a proposal is the grounding act in Clark and Brennan's [1991] sense, visible evidence that both parties share the same understanding. In conversation, grounding happens through backchannels and repairs. In a codebase where the other party is an agent, grounding requires a durable signal because silence is ambiguous between "I agree" and "I did not notice." That ambiguity is what lets intent debt accumulate. A verdict resolves it by recording agreement explicitly.

## 8.3 When a Map Fails

Three limitations replicated across both studies. Each traces to a structural property of hierarchical representation that cannot be designed away without abandoning the hierarchy itself.

The cross-cutting problem is the deepest. Parnas [1972] proved that any single decomposition principle makes some changes local and others non-local. "Every endpoint validates input the same way" is a property no single node in a capability tree can own. We considered multi-axis decomposition but that produces a lattice rather than a tree and sacrifices the structural uniformity that makes trust calibration possible. The design response is ephemeral queryable views that assemble a cross-cutting perspective on demand without adding permanent structure.

Debugging favors execution because the tree describes what the code is *for* rather than what it does step by step, and these are structurally different kinds of knowledge. Intent narrows the search space by identifying which features are candidates for the fault. Execution locates the fault within that space. The tree's contribution ends at narrowing, and participants abandoned it once they needed to trace state.

The initial mapping cost is the adoption threshold that Blackwell's [2002] attention investment model predicts. The mapping is not overhead separate from the threshold but the very process by which the threshold is crossed. A developer cannot calibrate trust without first reading enough of the tree to have something to verify. The five minutes spent reading and checking are not wasted even if the developer never returns, because those five minutes already produced the detection and decision outcomes measured in Section 7.3 and 7.5. The cost amortizes further on return visits, but the single-session payoff is sufficient on its own.

## 8.4 The Faithfulness-Visibility Tension

G1 and G2 cannot be maximized simultaneously. Any synchronized representation must choose where to put the boundary.

The tension is structural. A system that always updates descriptions to match code is always faithful but never surfaces changes a developer should question. A system that never updates preserves every historical commitment as a visible discrepancy but becomes unreliable within hours. Every intermediate position leaves some changes on the wrong side.

codoc's boundary is structural significance. A new module, a deleted feature, or a renamed responsibility changes the tree's shape and becomes a proposal requiring a verdict. A changed implementation under a stable interface, such as rewriting a loop to use a stream, changes the binding's content hash and produces a drift marker but does not demand an answer. We chose this boundary because it matches what we observed in Study 1 about what participants needed to notice. When P4 reported "it was hard to track impact on the final code," the changes that went unnoticed were structural rather than mechanical. Nobody complained about missing a variable rename. They complained about missing a responsibility that moved.

The design decision we rejected was a notification threshold based on diff size. A one-line change that moves a timeout from 30 seconds to 0 is more consequential than a 200-line refactoring that changes no behavior. The structural boundary catches the first because the feature's description contradicts its implementation. The diff-size boundary misses it because one line is below any reasonable threshold.

The change ledger records every auto-applied update regardless of the boundary, so the record is always complete even when the surface is selective. Whether the boundary is optimal reduces to an asymmetry of failure modes. Proposing too much trains the developer to accept without reading, the rubber-stamping failure Monperrus [2026] identified. Absorbing too much hides changes until something breaks. Section 7.3 reports one instance where the loop absorbed a change that should have been proposed, confirming the boundary is imperfect but identifying where it fails.

## 8.5 Implications for the Design of AI Coding Tools

The verification cost problem is not hypothetical. Faros AI [2025] reports that teams with high AI adoption saw code review time increase 91% and bug rates rise 9% while PRs merged nearly doubled in volume. Monperrus [2026] argues the logical endpoint is that "reviews of agent-generated code become rubber-stamps." Both overload and rubber-stamping are rational responses to a cost-benefit imbalance where the expected cost of checking exceeds the expected value of catching an error. The trust threshold suggests a different framing. The problem is not that developers lack diligence but that current tools make verification expensive enough to skip. Every implication below follows from a single principle: reduce the cost of one verification act below the threshold at which checking becomes the default behavior rather than the exception.

**The agent's output should include a maintained representation alongside the code.** Oukay et al. [2026] found that 58% of human effort in agent-authored pull requests is guidance-level intervention, expressing what the code should be rather than changing what it is. A persistent representation of intent is the surface through which that majority of oversight work can happen at the level developers actually think in.

**Proposals should live in the representation rather than in a separate approval queue.** A proposal shown in context carries its own evidence because the surrounding descriptions provide the standard against which the change is judged. A proposal in a queue requires the developer to reconstruct context before deciding, and reconstruction is the same expense that made verification costly in the first place.

**"Being made to decide" is a feature.** Norman [2013] calls this a *forcing function*. Schmalbach [2026] confirms independently that documentation appeared "mostly or only when explicitly demanded" by the contract structure. The durable trace finding from Section 7.5 reflects not better decisions but decisions at all.

Shukla et al. [2026] propose dynamic autonomy, where the agent adjusts permission-seeking as trust evolves. codoc addresses the complementary problem, making verification cheap enough that trust forms rapidly. The two compose because they operate on different variables. Dynamic autonomy reduces the volume of decisions an agent presents. codoc reduces the cost of each decision presented. Neither alone solves the scaling problem, because without cheap verification, dynamic autonomy simply defers the same expensive checks to fewer but more consequential moments.

A simpler intervention would highlight which sections of CLAUDE.md changed since the developer last read it. But a diff-highlight still requires voluntary encounter, and the detection findings suggest that forcing a decision produces outcomes that availability alone does not.

# 9. Limitations and Future Work

## 9.1 Limitations

**Single session.** Each participant worked with codoc for twenty minutes on an unfamiliar project. The representation's value is amortization. Murphy-Hill et al. [2012] found near-zero adoption of tools with proven long-term benefit because the benefit was invisible in the study window, and Stray et al. [2026] required two years to detect that Copilot showed no statistically significant productivity change despite users perceiving themselves faster. A single session captures the cost without demonstrating the compounding payoff.

**Twelve participants.** The confidence intervals are wide. The sign test has reasonable power for a consistent directional effect but cannot estimate magnitude tightly. The qualitative findings are richer than the numbers.

**Recorded session.** Everyone reviews the same change, ensuring comparability but sacrificing the ecological validity of watching their own agent work and interrupting mid-change.

**Constructed stimulus.** Planted problems were authored by experimenters and the recording was steered to produce them. codoc's response through proposals, amendments, and bindings was produced by a real daemon. If it failed to surface a problem, that failure is reported.

**Demand characteristics.** The briefing explaining synchronization may have primed trust formation. The think-aloud protocol may sharpen the apparent binary nature of the threshold, since narrating a verification decision imposes discrete narrative structure on a gradual reduction. The study cannot exclude these alternatives.

**Order effects on qualitative themes.** Counterbalancing controls for order in the quantitative measures, but qualitative themes are not averageable. A participant who uses codoc first brings structural vocabulary to the baseline condition. A participant who uses baseline first arrives at codoc already frustrated with step-by-step instructions. The themes reported in Section 7 may be shaped by what participants saw in the other condition rather than by the condition itself. The quantitative findings do not depend on qualitative interpretation, but the narrative coherence of the paper does.

**Bundle comparison.** codoc is a bundle of mechanisms and this study cannot attribute the effect to any single one. Ablation requires more conditions than twelve participants can support.

**Threshold characterization.** We report that the threshold forms after two or three verified features but cannot yet characterize what properties make a feature's verification conclusive. The architecture predicts that tight bindings to bounded code regions are verifiable while vague descriptions or diffuse bindings are not.

**Contamination untested.** The argument that a single stale description poisons trust in the entire artifact is grounded in Lee and See's recovery asymmetry and Schemmer's behavioral trust model, but no participant in this study encountered a stale description during the task. The one instance where Loop A absorbed a change silently tested the faithfulness-visibility tension rather than the contamination property. The fragility of the threshold remains a theoretical prediction rather than an observed result, and the engineering investment in continuous maintenance may exceed what a simpler tool would require if real-world trust is more robust to occasional inaccuracy than the model predicts.

**Scale.** The evaluation tested a representation of a small project, approximately 4000 lines with a feature tree that fits on two pages. The claim that the representation amortizes comprehension cost depends on the tree remaining surveyable. At 200 features, surveyability breaks. The design may require search, filtering, or scoped views to function at that scale.

## 9.2 Future Work

**Longitudinal deployment.** The trust threshold predicts compounding across sessions, but only if the representation remains faithful overnight. The critical question is whether returning developers skip re-verification entirely or whether each session opens with a brief recalibration. Session-scoped trust would substantially reduce the amortization benefit. Distinguishing the two requires instrumented daily use over at least four weeks.

**Multi-user coordination.** A team-maintained tree becomes a coordination mechanism, but a tree that another person changed without explanation undermines the uniformity that makes induction rational. Whether the trust threshold can form when multiple writers share the representation is an open question that the single-developer evaluation cannot address.

**Finer-grained bindings.** The current granularity is the function. Finer bindings would surface more precise proposals but multiply drift events, risking violation of the cheapness condition at the session level even while satisfying it per individual check. The trade-off is that of granularity against cognitive load, and the boundary likely depends on codebase maturity.

**Integration with version control.** Connecting the change ledger to git history so that `git blame` traces through the directive, the feature description, and the person who authored it would close the provenance loop entirely.

# 10. Conclusion

The developer from the introduction comes back the next morning to an agent that changed fourteen files. With codoc, the feature tree shows two proposals. One says the agent added a configuration layer and asks whether this new capability belongs in the system. The other says the timeout function was absorbed into the generic config and asks whether the specific values chosen after a production incident should be preserved. The developer accepts the first and rejects the second. Both decisions are recorded, attributable, and auditable. The timeout values survive. The next morning, the tree still maps to the code, and the developer still knows what the system is.

The evaluation suggests why. The mechanism is not any individual feature of the representation but the trust it earns through faithful synchronization. That trust is fragile, rapid in formation, and once established, restructures the entire relationship between developer and tool from exhaustive verification to targeted inspection.

The finding challenges a widespread assumption in developer tool design. The prevailing approach treats expressivity as the primary axis of investment. The trust threshold inverts this priority. A tool that faithfully represents five things is worth more than one that expressively represents fifty, because without trust the developer must verify each claim independently. The constraint that matters is not "can the developer say what they mean" but "can the developer believe what they read." Literate programming failed because nobody trusted the expressions to remain accurate after someone else changed the code. Intentional software and projectional editors solved the trust problem by making intent primary but broke version control and thereby refused the integration problem. Each generation solved a different subset of the three properties and left the remainder as an adoption barrier. The verification crisis is not a problem of better models or smarter agents. It is a problem of representational infrastructure.

Designing for the trust threshold, investing in faithfulness over expressivity so that a small number of checks suffice where exhaustive verification cannot, is the paper's central recommendation for developer tool builders. The architectural logic, that verification cost is the binding constraint and that local, uniform, cheap verification lowers it, may apply beyond code intent to any persistent representation that a person must trust to be useful. That generalization is a structural argument from this one system instance rather than a demonstrated fact, and the paper does not claim otherwise. The narrower claim is specific to agent-era code understanding, where representations must persist across sessions and stay faithful to a changing codebase. For that domain, the three conditions are testable predictions derived from this architecture and confirmed in their joint effect by this evaluation.
